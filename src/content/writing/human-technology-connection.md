Before reading on, I'd like you to do one thing. If you're on your iPhone, scroll to the bottom of this page.

Notice how it gently stretches a little past the edge and then springs back? You may have seen this thousands of times without thinking about it. Yet you probably understood what it meant: you had reached the end of the page. No message needed, just a feeling.

That bounce is a small piece of interface design. Apple's [scrolling patent](https://patents.google.com/patent/US7469381B2/en), credited to Bas Ording, describes content moving beyond an edge and returning. To me, it's the kind of detail that makes technology disappear into your hands. As we move toward AI, smart glasses and voices in our ears, I think that matters: the best technology is often the kind you forget you're using.

The graphical user interface has a longer history than the devices we carry today. In the 1970s, researchers at Xerox PARC explored a different way of using computers through the [Xerox Alto](https://www.computerhistory.org/revolution/input-output/14/347). A screen could become a workspace, with graphics and a mouse to point at things. Apple's [Macintosh, introduced in 1984](https://www.computerhistory.org/revolution/artifact/303/1203), helped bring that approach to a wider audience.

For people accustomed to typing commands, this was a significant change. You could point to something you wanted to work with. Windows, icons and menus gave actions a visible place on the screen.

Think about the familiar symbols in the software you use. A floppy disk can still mean "Save", even to someone who has never used one. A house means "Home". A small printer means "Print". These symbols connect an action on a screen with something we recognise, or something we have learned to recognise.

Early iPhone apps leaned into that connection. The Calculator looked like a calculator; the Clock borrowed from a clock face. This approach is often called skeuomorphism: digital objects borrowing the appearance of physical ones. The familiar shape offered a clue about what to do.

Apple later moved toward a flatter visual style with [iOS 7 in 2013](https://www.apple.com/newsroom/2013/06/10Apple-Unveils-iOS-7/). The physical controls changed too. The [iPhone 7](https://www.apple.com/newsroom/2016/09/apple-introduces-iphone-7-iphone-7-plus/) replaced the mechanically clicking Home button with a solid-state button and tactile feedback from the Taptic Engine. With the [iPhone X](https://www.apple.com/newsroom/2017/09/the-future-is-here-iphone-x/), a swipe from the bottom took you home.

A button became a feeling, then a gesture. The action remained familiar even as the way of performing it changed.

That's why this history interests me. With AI, [Meta's smart glasses](https://www.meta.com/smart-glasses/) and [Apple Vision Pro](https://www.apple.com/apple-vision-pro/), we are exploring interactions beyond the phone screen. The devices still exist, but the interface may be something we speak to, look at or reach toward.

I don't know exactly what comes next. I do think voice and vision will be a big part of it. Systems such as OpenAI's [computer-using agent](https://openai.com/index/computer-using-agent/) show how software can act through an interface on our behalf. That opens up another question: how should we tell it what we want?

Would you say "close this tab" and "open that one", or would you simply point? Would describing every small action be more work than doing it yourself? Voice can be useful, but I don't think speaking our existing clicks aloud is the whole answer.

As developers, we need to think about how people interact with what we build. What tells someone that an action worked? What makes the next step obvious? How can they correct a mistake without having to understand the machinery underneath?

Even when we build small pieces of software for ourselves, those questions matter. I keep coming back to that little bounce at the bottom of a page. It asks so little of us, yet it tells us exactly what we need to know.
